Overview: what this whole thing is

We are teaching a robotic arm (NERO) to manipulate objects by imitating human demonstrations instead of being hand-programmed. The pipeline looks like this:

  1. CollectTeleoperated demonstrations: camera frames plus the arm's joint positions, recorded as episodes.
  2. Audit & cleanAudit the dataset, purge bad episodes, smooth noisy trajectories.
  3. TrainTrain an imitation-learning policy that maps camera images to the arm's next actions.
  4. EvaluateRun the policy on the real arm, measure success rate, feed failures back into data collection.

Our shortlist from the literature: ACT, Diffusion Policy, and NVIDIA's GR00T.

Training policies: ACT vs Diffusion vs GR00T

Summaries of the three papers on our reading list. Each was published and evaluated separately, on different robots and tasks, so their numbers are not directly comparable.

Lightweight

ACT

Action Chunking with Transformers. Zhao, Kumar, Levine, Finn (2023), from the ALOHA low-cost bimanual system.[2]

  • Predicts a chunk of the next k joint actions at once instead of one step at a time, which fights the compounding-error problem of imitation learning.[2]
  • Transformer over ResNet-18 camera features plus joint positions, with a latent style variable (CVAE).[2][3]
  • Around 80M parameters; the LeRobot docs describe training in a few hours on a single GPU and high success rates from about 50 demonstrations.[3]
  • In the ALOHA paper: 80 to 90% success on 6 fine tasks (such as slotting a battery or opening a cup) from about 10 minutes of demos.[2]
  • First-class support in Hugging Face LeRobot, which recommends it as a starting point.[3]

Distribution modelling

Diffusion Policy

Chi, Xu, Feng et al. (2023). The visuomotor policy as a conditional denoising diffusion process.[4]

  • Instead of outputting one action, it refines noise into an action sequence over several denoising steps, conditioned on camera images.[4]
  • Handles multimodal demonstrations (several valid ways to do a task) that break plain regression policies.[4]
  • Receding-horizon control: re-plans every step while keeping the motion temporally consistent.[4]
  • In its own benchmarks: an average improvement of 46.9% over prior state of the art across 15 tasks in 4 benchmark suites.[4]
  • Uses iterative denoising at inference time rather than a single forward pass.[4]

Foundation model

GR00T

NVIDIA Isaac GR00T N1 (2025), an open foundation model for generalist humanoid robots.[5]

  • Dual-system design: a vision-language model (System 2) reasons about the scene and instruction; a diffusion transformer (System 1) turns the plan into continuous actions.[5]
  • Cross-embodiment: one set of weights adapts to different robots through post-training on your own demos.[5]
  • Trained on a large humanoid dataset plus synthetic and internet-video data; part of the dataset is open on Hugging Face.[5]
  • NVIDIA reports GR00T N1 2B averaging 76.8% success on real-world GR-1 humanoid tasks with full training data (vendor-reported; different tasks from the other two papers).[5]
  • 2B+ parameters, far larger than ACT's ~80M, so running or fine-tuning it needs correspondingly more compute.[5][3]
